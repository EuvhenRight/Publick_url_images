# From "We Use AI" to "AI Made Us Money": A Value Audit Guide

Almost every company now claims to use AI. Far fewer can point to a dollar it earned. This guide turns the LinkedIn post's core test into a repeatable audit you can run on any AI initiative.

> **The core distinction:** "We tried AI somewhere" and "We made money from it" are two completely different bars. Adoption is easy. Redesigning the job around the tool is where the value hides.

---

## The 4-Step Value Test

### 1. Name the workflow you rebuilt (not the tool you sprinkled)

Write one sentence:

> "We rebuilt **[workflow]** so that **[step]** is now done by AI, which changed **[who does what]**."

**Sprinkle (weak):** "We added an AI summarizer button to our ticketing tool."

**Rebuild (strong):** "Tier-1 support tickets now route to an AI agent that resolves or escalates. Human agents only see escalations, so we re-scoped their role toward complex cases."

If you can't name what changed about *who does what*, you sprinkled.

### 2. Pre-register the target metric

Before looking at any results, write down:

- The single number this project was supposed to move.
- Its baseline value (last 90 days).
- The target and the deadline.

Pre-registration prevents the most common self-deception in AI programs: picking the metric that happened to improve *after* the fact.

### 3. Define the metric precisely

Vague metrics hide failure. In customer support especially, these three sound identical and are not:

| Term | What it actually means | How it can mislead |
|------|------------------------|--------------------|
| **Deflected** | AI answered so a human never got involved | Counts abandoned chats as "wins" |
| **Contained** | Interaction stayed inside the bot channel | User may have given up, not been helped |
| **Resolved** | The customer's underlying problem was fixed | The only one that maps to real value |

Always ask: *does this metric require the customer's problem to actually be solved?* If not, it can go up while satisfaction goes down.

### 4. Check durability over time

A demo week is not evidence. Track the metric across a real stretch (8-12 weeks minimum) and watch for:

- **Novelty decay** — usage spikes then fades once the demo excitement passes.
- **Silent fallback** — humans quietly take back the work when the AI struggles.
- **Metric drift** — the win narrows to one easy sub-segment.

## Why adoption and no-return coexist

Two widely-cited (second-hand, treat as rough) figures:

- ~88% of companies report using AI in at least one function (relayed from McKinsey's annual survey).
- ~95% of AI pilots showed no measurable financial return (relayed from an MIT study).

**Both can be true.** The vendor gets paid the moment you press start — you don't have to finish. A pilot can die quietly while the invoice clears. That's why adoption is cheap and value is rare.

> ⚠️ **On the numbers:** These statistics circulate through aggregators and blogs. Cite them as directional, not gospel, and link the original source when you can find it. Overstating them undercuts your own credibility.

---

## Reading a vendor directory correctly

A list of 92 AI companies is a **map of who is selling, not who is winning.** When evaluating any entry:

1. Separate the *capability* claim from a *customer outcome* claim.
2. Ask for a named metric, a baseline, and a time window — not a logo wall.
3. Distinguish "powers X" (infrastructure) from "improved X by Y%" (result).
4. Beware compound buzzwords ("agentic analytic conversational computer-vision AI") — breadth of labels rarely correlates with depth of value.

---

## One honest question to end on

> On your biggest AI project: did you rebuild a workflow, or just sprinkle a tool on top?

If the answer is "sprinkle," that's fine — but budget accordingly and stop calling it transformation.

# Depth + AI: A Career Strategy for the Split Job Market

A practical companion to the post "Same job title, two opposite job markets."
This guide turns the argument into steps you can act on this week.

---

## 1. The one chart that reframes the whole thing

Pin every tech job ad to its early-2020 level and call that **100** (an index, not a
headcount). Two lines then diverge:

| Role | Early 2020 (index) | Mid 2025 (index) | Change |
|------|-------------------:|-----------------:|-------:|
| Machine-learning engineer | 100 | ~159 | up ~59% |
| General software engineer | 100 | ~51 | down ~49% |

Source: Indeed's hiring research team (indexed job-postings data). Treat the exact
numbers as directional — the *shape* is the point, and the shape is a **split**, not a
shrink. Two roles that share a lineage (and often a title) are now living in opposite
markets.

**Sanity check on the scary narrative:** only about **5%** of 2025 layoff announcements
cited AI as a cause — and that is the companies' own stated reason, not verified proof.
"AI replaced us" is a smaller story than the feed suggests. Restructuring, over-hiring
corrections, and rate-driven belt-tightening explain far more.

> Takeaway: the market didn't get smaller. It got *sorted*. Generic advice averages the
> two lines together and is therefore wrong for both.

---

## 2. Why "just specialize in AI, fast" expires first

Specializing purely in an *AI title* is a bet on a category that history keeps folding
back into the generalist:

- **Database administrator** → absorbed into "backend engineer."
- **Front-end** and **back-end** → absorbed into "full-stack."
- **AI / ML engineer** → the next specialty on that conveyor belt.

A specialty buys you a **lead** (scarcity, pay premium, optionality) but not a **moat**.
The lead is real and worth taking — just don't mistake it for permanent. Skills that ride
on a *durable base* (a domain, a product, a system) outlast the label attached to them.

---

## 3. The strategy: depth first, AI bolted on

### Step 1 — Go deep on one real thing
Pick a domain, product, or system you understand cold: payments fraud, clinical
scheduling, warehouse logistics, ad auctions, a specific large codebase. Depth is what
doesn't get commoditized when the tooling changes, because it's about *judgment in
context*, not syntax.

### Step 2 — Bolt AI onto that depth
AI is spreading into nearly every job, and your current role is quietly adding "can use
AI" to its description. You don't need to become a researcher. You need to be the person
in your domain who can wield AI *well*: framing the problem, choosing the tool, judging
the output, and knowing when not to use it.

### Step 3 — Aim where the money already moved
The expensive, slow, valuable work has shifted from **building** models to **keeping them
running and trusted in production** — MLOps / deployment / evaluation / reliability
(roughly ~$165K/yr in the post's reference point). Creation is increasingly cheap;
deployment, monitoring, and trust are not. Skate to where the puck already is.

---

## 4. Depth vs. label: a quick decision test

Ask of any skill you're about to invest months in:

1. **Portability** — does it stay valuable if the current framework/model dies? (Depth =
   yes. Tool-of-the-month = no.)
2. **Context** — does its value come from understanding a domain, or from a generic
   how-to anyone can copy?
3. **Absorption risk** — in 3 years, is this a *job title* or a *checkbox on everyone's
   job*? Titles get folded in; judgment doesn't.

If a skill is portable, context-rich, and hard to absorb → it's depth. If it's a hot
label that could become a bullet point on every résumé → it's a lead, so take it, but
anchor it to depth.

Write down exactly two things (see `worksheet.md`):

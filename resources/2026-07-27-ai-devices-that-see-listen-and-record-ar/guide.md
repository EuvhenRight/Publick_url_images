# Recording, Wearables, and Consent: A Practical Guide

This guide turns the LinkedIn post's argument into something actionable: how to think about, and reduce, the privacy and legal risk of AI wearables that record audio and video.

> Disclaimer: This is an educational summary, not legal advice. Recording laws vary by state and change frequently. Consult a licensed attorney for specific situations.

## 1. Why wearables break the old model

For over a century, recording required a visible gesture: raising a camera or a phone. That gesture served as informal notice and often as a proxy for consent. Smart glasses remove the gesture. A camera and microphone can run continuously behind a small indicator light that bystanders rarely notice.

The key shift: **the person carrying the legal and privacy risk is often the bystander who never opted in.**

## 2. One-party vs. all-party consent (US)

Wiretapping/eavesdropping laws generally distinguish two regimes:

- **One-party consent**: Only one participant in a conversation needs to consent to its recording. This is the federal baseline and the rule in most states.
- **All-party ("two-party") consent**: Every participant in a private conversation must consent.

States commonly cited as all-party consent for private communications include:

| State | Notes |
|-------|-------|
| California | Penal Code § 632 — confidential communications |
| Illinois | Eavesdropping statute (720 ILCS 5/14-2) |
| Florida | § 934.03 |
| Pennsylvania | Wiretap Act |
| Washington | RCW 9.73 |
| Massachusetts | Notably strict; even secret recording of a party |
| Maryland | Basis of the famous Linda Tripp case |
| Michigan, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, Connecticut | Vary by interpretation / audio vs. in-person |

> The exact count of "all-party" states (often cited as ~11–12) depends on how you classify states with mixed or nuanced rules. Always check the current statute for the specific state.

Two important qualifiers:

1. **Reasonable expectation of privacy.** Most statutes only protect "private" or "confidential" communications. Recording in a public place where people don't expect privacy is treated very differently from recording a closed-door conversation.
2. **Audio vs. video.** Many strict consent laws target *audio* capture. Silent video in public may be legal even where audio recording is not.

## 3. The rules are lagging the devices

The post's central point: hardware ships years before regulation adapts. Existing wiretapping laws were written for phones and tape recorders, not always-on wearables. Purpose-built "wearable recording" legislation is still emerging and, in many places, not yet law. Until then, decades-old statutes get applied to new devices by analogy — creating uncertainty for wearers and bystanders alike.

## 4. Checklist: if you WEAR recording glasses

- Learn the consent regime of the state you're in **and** any state the other party is in (interstate calls can trigger the stricter law).
- Treat all-party states as: get explicit verbal agreement before recording private conversations.
- Announce recording out loud; don't rely on a small LED.
- Disable continuous transcription/audio in private spaces (homes, bathrooms, medical offices, locker rooms).
- Know that workplaces, courts, theaters, and many venues ban recording devices outright.
- Delete/limit retention; the more you store, the more you expose others.

## 5. Checklist: if you're a BYSTANDER

- You can ask someone to remove or power off a recording device around you; it's a reasonable request.
- In all-party states, non-consensual recording of your private conversation may be unlawful.
- Look for the indicator light, but don't assume its absence means no recording.
- For sensitive discussions, move to a private space and state that you do not consent to recording.

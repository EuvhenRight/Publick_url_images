# The Trades Pay Ladder: A Decision Guide

This guide unpacks the LinkedIn post's core argument — that "the trades" is not one job, and the eye-catching pay is gated behind years of licensing. Use it to evaluate the choice honestly instead of on the headline.

## 1. The three tiers hidden inside "the trades"

| Tier | Examples | Typical entry | Pay reality |
|------|----------|---------------|-------------|
| General labor | Helper, laborer, apprentice-year-1 | Immediate, no credential | Modest, hourly, often the "floor" |
| Licensed specialist | Electrician, plumber, HVAC tech | 3–5 yr paid apprenticeship + exams | High, but time-gated |
| Credentialed manager | Project manager, superintendent, estimator | Experience + PMP/OSHA/degree | Highest, smallest headcount |

The "six figures" headlines almost always describe the bottom two tiers, not someone starting this quarter.

## 2. Why high pay can't recruit its own fix quickly

- Licensing caps supply. You legally cannot work unsupervised until you pass exams and log required hours.
- Apprenticeship hours are fixed (commonly 6,000–8,000 hours = ~4 years).
- So a wage spike today rewards people who started ~5 years ago, not new entrants.
- This is a lagging supply curve: the market can't flood the gate on demand.

## 3. The honest timeline for a new entrant (electrician example)

```
Year 0     Enroll in apprenticeship, earn ~40-50% of journeyman wage
Year 1-4   Paid on-the-job hours + classroom, wage steps up each year
Year 4-5   Sit for journeyman exam
Year 5     Journeyman: the "six figure" tier becomes reachable
Year 7-10  Master license / supervisory / self-employed upside
```

The raise you read about lands around Year 5, not Year 0.

## 4. Trade vs. four-year degree: an honest comparison

| Factor | Skilled trade | Four-year degree |
|--------|---------------|------------------|
| Upfront debt | Low/none (paid apprenticeship) | Often significant |
| Time to income | Immediate (low) → 5 yr (high) | 4 yr, then entry job |
| Ceiling | High for licensed/owner, capped for labor | Wide range, field-dependent |
| Automation exposure | Low for hands-on work | Varies |
| Physical toll | Real, accumulates | Low |
| Geographic flexibility | License is often state-specific | Usually portable |

## 5. Questions to ask before you commit

1. Which tier am I actually targeting — laborer, licensed specialist, or manager?
2. Is there a funded apprenticeship in my state, and what are its exact hour/exam requirements?
3. Does my state license transfer if I move (reciprocity)?
4. Am I comparing the trade's Year-5 pay against a degree's Year-5 pay, or against its Year-0 pay?
5. What is the physical career length in this trade, and what's the exit path (management, ownership, inspection)?

## 6. The takeaway

The trades can be a genuinely strong bet — but the win goes to those who plan for the 5-year gate, pick a licensed specialty, and treat the apprenticeship as the investment that the degree crowd calls tuition. "It career" framing skips the gate; don't.

# Appearance Bias at Work: Practical Guardrails for Hiring and Reviews

This guide helps teams reduce appearance-based judgments in hiring, promotion, performance reviews, and customer-facing role decisions.

It is not legal advice. Employment law varies by location, and some appearance-related traits may overlap with legally protected characteristics such as race, sex, religion, disability, age, national origin, or gender identity. Consult qualified counsel for policy or compliance decisions.

## What appearance bias can look like

Appearance bias, sometimes called lookism, happens when people treat appearance as evidence of competence, professionalism, reliability, leadership potential, or cultural fit.

Common examples include assumptions based on:

- Hair texture, hairstyle, or grooming norms
- Weight, body shape, height, or perceived fitness
- Teeth, skin, visible disability, scars, acne, or aging
- Tattoos, piercings, makeup, clothing, or accessories
- Gender expression or conformity to traditional presentation norms
- Religious, cultural, or ethnic dress and hairstyles
- Perceived attractiveness or lack of attractiveness

## Red flags in workplace language

These phrases are not always discriminatory, but they can hide subjective appearance judgments:

- “They don’t look polished.”
- “Not executive presence.”
- “Not client-ready.”
- “Doesn’t fit our brand.”
- “Doesn’t look professional.”
- “Not the image we want.”
- “They need to clean up their look.”
- “They don’t look like leadership material.”

When these phrases appear, ask: **What job-related behavior are we actually describing?**

## Replace vague judgments with job-related evidence

| Vague appearance-based comment | Better job-related question |
|---|---|
| “They don’t look professional.” | Did they meet the stated dress, hygiene, or safety requirements for the role? |
| “They lack polish.” | Which communication, preparation, or stakeholder-management behavior was missing? |
| “They’re not client-ready.” | What client-facing competencies are required, and what evidence shows they lack them? |
| “They don’t fit our image.” | Is this image requirement essential to the job, consistently applied, and non-discriminatory? |
| “They don’t have executive presence.” | Which leadership behaviors were demonstrated or not demonstrated? |

## Hiring checklist

Before interviews:

- Define role requirements in writing before reviewing candidates.
- Separate required qualifications from preferences.
- Use structured interview questions tied to competencies.
- Create a scoring rubric before interviews begin.
- Avoid evaluating social media photos unless clearly job-related and consistently applied.
- Train interviewers to document evidence, not impressions.

During interviews:

- Ask the same core questions to each candidate.
- Score answers against the rubric immediately after the interview.
- Do not discuss attractiveness, weight, age appearance, hair, clothing style, tattoos, or “vibe” unless directly tied to a legitimate written requirement.
- If dress or grooming is relevant, evaluate only against the stated standard.

After interviews:

- Require interviewers to cite specific evidence for each score.
- Challenge comments like “not polished,” “not a fit,” or “not client-ready.”
- Compare candidates on role criteria, not comfort, similarity, or presentation norms.
- Audit whether certain appearance-related traits correlate with lower scores.

## Performance and promotion checklist

- Define promotion criteria before discussing candidates.
- Ask managers to provide examples of outcomes, behaviors, and impact.
- Review whether “executive presence” is being used as a proxy for appearance, accent, gender expression, age, body type, or class background.
- Separate communication skill from style preference.
- Do not penalize employees for appearance traits unrelated to job performance.
- Apply dress, grooming, and customer-facing standards consistently.
- Provide clear, behavior-based feedback rather than image-based feedback.

## Customer-facing roles

Some roles involve uniforms, safety requirements, hygiene standards, or brand presentation. These requirements should be:

1. Written down
2. Job-related
3. Consistently applied
4. As narrow as possible
5. Reviewed for disparate impact
6. Flexible enough to accommodate disability, religion, pregnancy, culture, and other protected needs where required by law

A useful test: **Would we enforce this standard the same way for every employee with the same job duties?**

## Questions to ask when appearance comes up

- What specific job requirement is involved?
- Is this requirement written in the job description, policy, or rubric?
- Is the standard necessary for safety, hygiene, legal compliance, or essential job performance?
- Are we applying the standard consistently?
- Could the concern be addressed through a neutral uniform, dress code, or communication expectation?
- Could this judgment disadvantage people based on race, sex, religion, disability, age, gender expression, socioeconomic background, or body size?
- What evidence would change our mind?

## A simple meeting script

When someone says: “They just don’t look professional.”

Try:

> “Let’s translate that into job-related criteria. Are we talking about a specific dress code issue, a communication issue, preparation, or something else? What evidence do we have, and is that standard written and applied to everyone?”

When someone says: “They’re not client-ready.”

Try:

> “Which client-facing competency are we assessing: responsiveness, product knowledge, judgment, communication clarity, or relationship management? Let’s score that directly rather than relying on general impressions.”

## Minimal policy language to consider

> Employment decisions should be based on job-related qualifications, performance, conduct, and business needs. Appearance-related standards must be role-specific, consistently applied, and connected to legitimate requirements such as safety, hygiene, uniform use, or essential customer-facing duties. Managers should avoid using subjective terms such as “polish,” “fit,” “image,” or “executive presence” unless they can explain the specific job-related behavior being evaluated.

## Quick self-audit

For the last hiring, promotion, or performance cycle, review notes for terms such as:

- polished
- professional look
- image
- fit
- executive presence
- client-ready
- youthful
- energetic
- well-groomed
- intimidating
- sloppy
- attractive
- clean-cut

For each term, ask whether it was supported by job-related evidence. If not, revise the process before the next cycle.
